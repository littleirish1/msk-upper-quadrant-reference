# Elbow Case 01: Sudden Antecubital Pain

## Case presentation

Mark is a 49-year-old patient who presents with sudden pain in the front of the elbow after lifting a heavy box yesterday.

He felt a distinct â€œpopâ€ in the antecubital fossa at the time of injury. Over the past 24 hours, bruising has developed around the front of the elbow. He reports weakness when turning the palm upwards and when bending the elbow.

Pain is 5/10 at rest and increases to 8/10 with lifting or resisted supination. He wants to know whether this needs urgent onward referral.

<ReasoningPrompt question="What is your leading clinical hypothesis, and what features support it?" />

<RevealAnswer>
The leading hypothesis is a complete distal biceps tendon rupture.

Supporting features include sudden onset during heavy lifting, a clear â€œpopâ€ in the antecubital fossa, bruising, pain around the distal biceps region, weakness with elbow flexion, and especially weakness with supination.

Supination weakness is a key feature because the biceps is a major contributor to forearm supination. In this mechanism, marked supination weakness should raise strong suspicion of distal biceps rupture until proven otherwise.
</RevealAnswer>

## Differential diagnosis

<ReasoningPrompt question="What are your top differentials, and what would make each more or less likely?" />

<RevealAnswer>
Important differentials include distal biceps rupture, biceps strain, elbow sprain, contusion, radial head injury, and other acute soft tissue injury.

A simple strain or contusion becomes less likely when there is a clear pop, visible bruising, a possible palpable gap, and marked supination weakness.

A radial head injury may cause pain after trauma and limitation of forearm rotation, but the mechanism, bruising pattern, antecubital tenderness, and positive hook test point more strongly toward distal biceps rupture.

A partial distal biceps tear remains possible, but the inability to hook the tendon and the functional loss make complete rupture a major concern.
</RevealAnswer>

## Red flag and referral reasoning

<ReasoningPrompt question="Is this an urgent referral, routine referral, or conservative management case?" />

<RevealAnswer>
This should be treated as an urgent onward referral.

A suspected complete distal biceps rupture is time-critical because early orthopaedic assessment is needed to determine whether surgical repair is appropriate. Primary repair is usually more straightforward earlier, before significant tendon retraction and fibrosis occur.

The key decision is not simply â€œwhat exercise should he do?â€ but whether the patient needs urgent upper-limb orthopaedic review.
</RevealAnswer>

<RedFlag title="Time-critical referral">
Suspected complete distal biceps rupture should be referred urgently for upper-limb orthopaedic assessment, especially when there is acute onset, bruising, marked supination weakness, and a positive hook test.
</RedFlag>

## Objective assessment

<ReasoningPrompt question="What would you prioritise in your objective assessment?" />

<RevealAnswer>
Priorities include observation for bruising and deformity, palpation of the antecubital fossa and distal biceps tendon, active elbow flexion and extension, active pronation and supination, resisted elbow flexion, resisted supination, neurovascular screening, and appropriate special testing such as the hook test.

In this case, the positive hook test is highly concerning: the examiner is unable to hook a cord-like distal biceps tendon from the lateral side, which supports complete rupture.

X-ray may help exclude fracture but does not rule out tendon rupture.
</RevealAnswer>

## Expected findings

<ReasoningPrompt question="What findings would fit a likely complete distal biceps rupture?" />

<RevealAnswer>
Expected findings include ecchymosis around the antecubital fossa, local tenderness, possible soft tissue asymmetry, possible palpable gap, painful but possible elbow flexion, marked weakness with supination, and a positive hook test.

The most important functional deficit is often supination weakness. Elbow flexion may remain possible because other muscles can contribute, so preserved flexion does not rule out rupture.
</RevealAnswer>

## Management reasoning

<ReasoningPrompt question="What would your immediate management focus on?" />

<RevealAnswer>
Immediate management should focus on protecting the injury, avoiding heavy lifting and forceful supination, providing clear education, and arranging urgent onward referral.

This is not a case for a trial of routine strengthening first. The clinical priority is timely surgical opinion because management decisions are time-sensitive.

If there are signs of neurovascular compromise, severe escalating pain, or other concerning trauma features, escalation should be more urgent.
</RevealAnswer>

## Common traps

<RevealAnswer title="Common traps in this case">
Common traps include treating the presentation as a simple sprain or contusion, missing the significance of supination weakness, failing to ask about the pop and mechanism, not checking for bruising or a palpable tendon defect, and not recognising the time-critical referral window.

Another trap is over-relying on one test. A positive hook test strongly supports rupture, but a negative or unclear test should not automatically exclude rupture if the history and functional deficit remain concerning.
</RevealAnswer>
