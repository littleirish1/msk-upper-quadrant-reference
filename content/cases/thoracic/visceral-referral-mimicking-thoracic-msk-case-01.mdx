# Thoracic Case 01: Visceral Referral Mimicking MSK Pain

## Case presentation

Michael is a 55-year-old accountant who presents with mid-back pain that he initially assumed was postural.

He reports a deep ache around the mid-thoracic region. The pain has been present for several weeks and does not clearly change with movement, position, spinal loading, or rest. He has tried stretching, heat, and changing his workstation setup, but symptoms have not meaningfully changed.

On further questioning, he reports associated upper abdominal discomfort and intermittent nausea. Symptoms sometimes feel worse after meals. He is worried because the pain feels different from previous episodes of mechanical back pain.

<ReasoningPrompt question="What is your leading concern, and why should this not be treated as straightforward MSK thoracic pain?" />

<RevealAnswer>
The leading concern is visceral referral mimicking thoracic MSK pain.

The key issue is that the pain does not behave mechanically. It does not clearly change with movement, position, spinal loading, or rest. The associated upper abdominal symptoms and nausea make a non-MSK source more likely.

Thoracic pain can be mechanical, but when the symptom behaviour is non-mechanical and associated with systemic, gastrointestinal, cardiac, pulmonary, or other visceral features, the visceral possibility needs to be actively considered rather than dismissed.
</RevealAnswer>

## Why MSK is tempting

<ReasoningPrompt question="Why might a clinician initially anchor on a musculoskeletal explanation here?" />

<RevealAnswer>
MSK anchoring is tempting because the patient describes pain in the mid-back region and links it to desk work and posture.

However, a partial MSK link does not prove an MSK cause. Many patients will naturally attribute thoracic symptoms to posture, lifting, stress, or work position. The clinicianâ€™s job is to test whether the symptom behaviour actually fits a mechanical pattern.

In this case, the lack of clear mechanical aggravating and easing factors makes a simple postural explanation less convincing.
</RevealAnswer>

## Features that do not fit MSK

<ReasoningPrompt question="Which features argue against a simple mechanical thoracic presentation?" />

<RevealAnswer>
Features arguing against a simple MSK presentation include pain that does not clearly change with spinal movement or position, poor response to typical self-management, associated upper abdominal discomfort, intermittent nausea, possible meal-related symptoms, and the patient reporting that this feels different from previous mechanical pain.

These features do not diagnose a specific visceral condition, but they are enough to justify further medical assessment rather than routine MSK treatment alone.
</RevealAnswer>

## Visceral referral patterns

<ReasoningPrompt question="What visceral systems would you keep in mind with thoracic or mid-back pain?" />

<RevealAnswer>
Important visceral considerations can include cardiac, pulmonary, gastrointestinal, hepatobiliary, renal, and vascular sources.

The exact suspicion depends on the history. For example, chest symptoms, exertional symptoms, shortness of breath, sweating, or cardiovascular risk factors would raise concern for cardiac causes. Cough, fever, breathlessness, or pleuritic symptoms may suggest pulmonary involvement. Upper abdominal symptoms, nausea, reflux-type symptoms, or meal-related patterns may point toward gastrointestinal or hepatobiliary sources.

The key reasoning point is not to memorise one referral map, but to notice when the pain behaviour is not mechanically consistent.
</RevealAnswer>

## Red flag and referral reasoning

<ReasoningPrompt question="What red flags or referral features would you screen for?" />

<RevealAnswer>
Screen for chest pain, shortness of breath, sweating, dizziness, exertional symptoms, fever, unexplained weight loss, night sweats, persistent night pain, history of cancer, abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, altered bowel habits, urinary symptoms, neurological signs, trauma, and vascular risk factors.

In this case, associated upper abdominal symptoms and nausea alongside non-mechanical thoracic pain mean the safest decision is onward medical assessment. The exact urgency depends on the severity and nature of symptoms, but this should not be managed as routine MSK thoracic pain without medical review.
</RevealAnswer>

## Objective assessment boundaries

<ReasoningPrompt question="Would you still perform an MSK objective assessment?" />

<RevealAnswer>
A limited MSK assessment may be appropriate if the patient is stable and there are no emergency features, but the assessment should not be used to force an MSK diagnosis.

Useful checks may include thoracic active range of movement, symptom response to repeated movement, palpation, rib/thoracic loading, neurological screen where relevant, and basic observations.

However, if symptoms are clearly non-mechanical or concerning systemic/visceral features are present, the priority is referral or medical review rather than prolonged MSK testing.
</RevealAnswer>

## Referral decision

<ReasoningPrompt question="What would your management decision be at this point?" />

<RevealAnswer>
The safest management decision is to refer for medical assessment rather than continue with routine MSK treatment.

This does not mean a serious condition is confirmed. It means the presentation does not fit a confident MSK pattern and includes features that require exclusion of visceral or systemic causes.

If symptoms suggest acute cardiac, vascular, severe abdominal, or respiratory compromise, emergency escalation is required. If symptoms are less acute but still concerning, same-day or urgent GP/medical review may be appropriate depending on local pathways.
</RevealAnswer>

## Common traps

<RevealAnswer title="Common traps in this case">
Common traps include anchoring on posture, assuming all thoracic pain is mechanical, over-valuing a partial MSK trigger, ignoring symptoms that do not change with movement, and failing to ask about abdominal, chest, pulmonary, systemic, or vascular features.

Another trap is giving exercises or manual therapy before the non-MSK possibilities have been appropriately considered.
</RevealAnswer>
