# Thoracic Case 01: Visceral Referral Mimicking MSK Pain

## Case presentation

Michael is a 55-year-old accountant who presents with left-sided upper thoracic and periscapular discomfort that he initially assumed was postural.

He has been working long hours at a desk during year-end accounts. He also returned to light gym work two weeks ago and noticed his upper back felt tight after rowing and pressing exercises. These details make an MSK explanation tempting.

The symptom he is worried about is a deep ache between the left shoulder blade and spine, sometimes spreading as a band-like pressure across the front of the chest. It is not reliably reproduced by thoracic rotation, shoulder movement, palpation, resisted testing, or loaded spinal positions. Heat and stretching help general stiffness but do not change the main symptom.

The most concerning episode occurred while walking uphill from a car park. He developed the same thoracic/scapular ache with chest pressure, felt clammy and mildly nauseous, and had to stop. Symptoms settled after several minutes of rest. A milder episode occurred again on stairs the following day.

He has hypertension, previously raised cholesterol, a past smoking history, and a family history of myocardial infarction in a first-degree relative in their late 50s. He denies cough, fever, calf pain, pleuritic pain, unexplained weight loss, night sweats, trauma, neurological symptoms, or bowel/bladder disturbance.

<ReasoningPrompt question="What initially looks MSK, and what does not fit a simple mechanical explanation?" />

<RevealAnswer>
The MSK red herrings are plausible: desk work, upper back tightness, recent gym loading, and tenderness around the thoracic/periscapular region.

The features that do not fit simple mechanical thoracic pain are more important: exertional onset, chest pressure, clamminess, nausea, symptoms easing with rest, cardiovascular risk factors, and poor reproduction with thoracic or shoulder testing.

This pattern raises concern for a possible cardiac-related source presenting with thoracic or scapular discomfort. The website cannot diagnose that, but the presentation requires urgent medical screening before routine MSK management.
</RevealAnswer>

## Targeted screening questions

<ReasoningPrompt question="Which two targeted questions would most change your risk assessment?" />

<RevealAnswer>
High-value questions include:

- Do symptoms come on with exertion and ease with rest?
- Is there chest pressure, breathlessness, sweating, nausea, dizziness, or a sense of heaviness?
- Are there cardiovascular risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, smoking history, high cholesterol, or family history?
- Are there pulmonary clues such as cough, fever, pleuritic pain, or calf pain?
- Are there systemic features such as unexplained weight loss, night sweats, or persistent night pain?

In this case, the exertional pattern, chest pressure, nausea/clamminess, and cardiovascular risk profile are the most important details because they change the safety decision.
</RevealAnswer>

## Features that increase concern

<ReasoningPrompt question="Which two red flags or caution features would stop you treating this as routine MSK pain today?" />

<RevealAnswer>
The most important caution features are symptoms provoked by walking uphill or stairs, associated chest pressure, clamminess, nausea, and cardiovascular risk factors.

The lack of clear reproduction with thoracic or shoulder examination also matters. MSK findings such as stiffness or local tenderness may coexist, but they do not explain the exertional and autonomic features.

This does not confirm a cardiac diagnosis. It means the presentation is not safe for routine MSK care until a time-sensitive medical cause has been excluded.
</RevealAnswer>

## What would reduce concern?

<ReasoningPrompt question="What findings would support an MSK explanation, and what findings would reduce but not remove medical concern?" />

<RevealAnswer>
An MSK explanation would be more convincing if the main symptom were consistently reproduced by thoracic movement, shoulder loading, palpation, rib provocation, or specific postures, and if it changed predictably with mechanical loading and easing strategies.

Concern would be reduced if there were no exertional pattern, no chest pressure, no breathlessness, no nausea/clamminess, no relevant vascular risk factors, and a clear mechanical reproduction of the primary symptom.

Even then, clinical judgement and local pathways matter. The presence of exertional symptoms with chest pressure should not be dismissed because an upper back area is tender.
</RevealAnswer>

## Objective assessment boundaries

<ReasoningPrompt question="Would you continue with an MSK objective assessment?" />

<RevealAnswer>
A brief observation and limited MSK screen may be reasonable if the patient is stable, but prolonged MSK testing is not the priority once exertional chest pressure, nausea/clamminess, and cardiovascular risk factors emerge.

The objective assessment should not be used to force the presentation into an MSK category. The safer reasoning is to stop, reassess risk, and arrange appropriate medical screening.

If symptoms are current, severe, escalating, associated with breathlessness, faintness, marked sweating, or other emergency features, escalation should be immediate according to local emergency pathways.
</RevealAnswer>

<RedFlag title="Exertional thoracic/scapular pain with chest pressure">
Thoracic or scapular pain that is provoked by exertion and associated with chest pressure, sweating, nausea, breathlessness, dizziness, or cardiovascular risk factors should be treated as a medical screening priority rather than routine MSK pain.
</RedFlag>

## Referral decision

<ReasoningPrompt question="What is the safest action or referral decision?" />

<RevealAnswer>
The safest decision is urgent medical assessment rather than routine MSK management.

This is not because the clinician has confirmed a diagnosis. It is because the pattern raises concern for cardiac-related referred pain and includes features that could represent time-sensitive pathology.

If symptoms are active or severe, emergency escalation is appropriate. If symptoms have settled but the exertional pattern and risk profile are clear, same-day urgent medical review is still more appropriate than exercises, manual therapy, or reassurance alone.
</RevealAnswer>

## Communication

<ReasoningPrompt question="How would you explain the decision without alarming the patient unnecessarily?" />

<RevealAnswer>
A balanced explanation could be:

"Some upper back pain does come from muscles, joints, ribs, or posture. You do have a few features that could fit that. The part I do not want to ignore is that your main symptom came on with exertion, spread toward the chest, and came with nausea and clamminess. Because of that pattern, it is safest to get medical screening before we treat this as a routine MSK problem."
</RevealAnswer>

## Common traps

<RevealAnswer title="Common traps in this case">
Common traps include anchoring on desk posture, over-valuing local thoracic tenderness, assuming a gym trigger explains all symptoms, failing to ask about exertion, and treating chest pressure as irrelevant because the patient describes the main area as "upper back".

Another trap is continuing with exercises or manual therapy after safety information has changed the risk profile. The reasoning task is to recognise when the story stops behaving like straightforward MSK pain.
</RevealAnswer>
