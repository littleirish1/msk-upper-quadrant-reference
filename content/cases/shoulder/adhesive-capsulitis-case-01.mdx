# Shoulder Case 02: Progressive Shoulder Stiffness

## Case presentation

A 56-year-old patient with type 2 diabetes presents with an 8-month history of progressive left shoulder stiffness.

The shoulder was initially very painful, especially at night, but the pain has reduced slightly. Stiffness is now the dominant problem. She struggles to reach behind her back, reach across her body, dress normally, and sleep comfortably.

There was no trauma or dislocation. She previously attended physiotherapy elsewhere, mainly involving deep tissue massage, but she reports no meaningful improvement. She is frustrated and asks, “Will this ever get better?”

<ReasoningPrompt question="What is your leading clinical hypothesis, and what features support it?" />

<RevealAnswer>
The leading hypothesis is adhesive capsulitis.

Supporting features include progressive shoulder stiffness, a prolonged time course, initial high pain followed by stiffness becoming dominant, difficulty reaching behind the back, difficulty dressing, night pain, no trauma, and the presence of type 2 diabetes.

The key pattern to look for is restriction of both active and passive glenohumeral range, especially external rotation.
</RevealAnswer>

## Differential diagnosis

<ReasoningPrompt question="What are your top three differentials, and what would make each more or less likely?" />

<RevealAnswer>
Important differentials include rotator cuff related shoulder pain, glenohumeral osteoarthritis, cervical radiculopathy, and significant rotator cuff tear.

Rotator cuff related shoulder pain may cause lateral shoulder pain and night pain, but passive range is usually relatively preserved compared with adhesive capsulitis.

Glenohumeral osteoarthritis can cause stiffness and pain, but may be associated with older age, crepitus, radiographic change, and a different pattern of joint symptoms.

Cervical radiculopathy becomes more likely if there is neck pain, arm pain beyond the shoulder, paraesthesia, weakness, reflex changes, or a dermatomal pattern.

A significant rotator cuff tear becomes more likely with trauma, marked weakness, loss of active movement with relatively preserved passive movement, or a positive lag sign.
</RevealAnswer>

## Red flag screening

<ReasoningPrompt question="What red flags or serious pathology considerations would you screen for?" />

<RevealAnswer>
Screen for unexplained weight loss, night sweats, fever, history of cancer, constant unremitting pain, unexplained systemic symptoms, recent infection, significant trauma, fracture risk, and progressive neurological symptoms.

Although this presentation is consistent with adhesive capsulitis, persistent night pain and significant functional loss should still be interpreted in context and monitored.
</RevealAnswer>

## Objective assessment

<ReasoningPrompt question="What would you prioritise in your objective assessment?" />

<RevealAnswer>
Prioritise active and passive shoulder range of movement, especially external rotation, abduction, flexion, hand-behind-back, and cross-body movement.

Compare active and passive range. Adhesive capsulitis usually presents with restriction in both.

Also assess irritability, pain behaviour, cervical screen if symptoms suggest referral, resisted testing where tolerated, functional tasks, and baseline outcome measures.
</RevealAnswer>

## Expected findings

<ReasoningPrompt question="What objective findings would fit your leading hypothesis?" />

<RevealAnswer>
Expected findings would include a capsular pattern of restriction, with external rotation commonly most limited. Active and passive movement are both restricted.

There may be pain at end range, guarding depending on irritability, difficulty with hand-behind-back, and reduced functional reach.

Strength testing may be painful or limited by range, but the defining feature is global passive restriction rather than isolated weakness.
</RevealAnswer>

## Management reasoning

<ReasoningPrompt question="What would your early management focus on, and why?" />

<RevealAnswer>
Early management should focus on education, reassurance, explaining the likely natural history, symptom-guided mobility work, maintaining function, and avoiding overly aggressive treatment that flares symptoms.

Exercise should match irritability. In a highly irritable painful phase, gentle mobility and pain management may be more appropriate. In a stiffer less irritable phase, progressive stretching, strengthening within available range, and functional restoration become more relevant.

Because Diane is frustrated, it is important to validate the impact, explain that recovery can be slow, and set realistic expectations.
</RevealAnswer>

## Common traps

<RevealAnswer title="Common traps in this case">
Common traps include assuming all shoulder pain with night pain is rotator cuff related, missing the importance of passive range restriction, treating stiffness too aggressively, ignoring diabetes as a relevant risk factor, and failing to explain the expected time course.

Another trap is continuing passive massage-based treatment without addressing education, range, function, self-management, and staged progression.
</RevealAnswer>
