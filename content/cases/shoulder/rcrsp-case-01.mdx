# Shoulder Case 01: Lateral Shoulder Pain

A 29-year-old active patient presents with a 5-month history of right lateral shoulder pain. Symptoms began gradually with no clear traumatic event. Pain is worse with overhead activity, throwing, and lying on the affected side at night.

They have tried rest for 6 weeks with minimal improvement. NSAIDs from the GP gave some benefit. They are motivated to return to sport.

<ReasoningPrompt question="What is your leading clinical hypothesis, and what features support it?" />

<RevealAnswer>
The leading hypothesis is rotator cuff related shoulder pain.

Supporting features include lateral deltoid-region pain, pain with overhead activity, pain with throwing, night pain when lying on the affected side, gradual onset, and partial response to NSAIDs. The presentation is load-related and does not strongly suggest instability or acute traumatic tear.
</RevealAnswer>

<ReasoningPrompt question="What are three important differentials you would keep in mind?" />

<RevealAnswer>
Reasonable differentials include frozen shoulder, cervical radiculopathy, AC joint pain, shoulder instability, and a significant rotator cuff tear. Frozen shoulder is less likely if passive range is preserved. Cervical radiculopathy becomes more likely if there are neck symptoms, dermatomal pain, paraesthesia, weakness, reflex changes, or positive neurodynamic findings.
</RevealAnswer>

<ReasoningPrompt question="What would you want to assess objectively?" />

<RevealAnswer>
Useful objective assessment would include active and passive shoulder range of movement, painful arc, resisted external rotation, resisted abduction, scapular control, cervical screening, neurological screen if indicated, and functional testing relevant to throwing or overhead activity.
</RevealAnswer>

<ReasoningPrompt question="What would your early management focus on?" />

<RevealAnswer>
Early management would usually focus on education, load modification, graded strengthening, restoring confidence with movement, sport-specific progression, and monitoring irritability. Management should be adapted depending on pain severity, strength deficits, functional goals, and whether symptoms suggest another diagnosis.
</RevealAnswer>
