# Cervical Case 02: Subtle Hand Dysfunction and Heavy Legs

## Case presentation

Patricia is a 58-year-old retired nurse referred for a pre-operative physiotherapy assessment before planned bilateral carpal tunnel release.

She reports a 6-month history of increasing difficulty with fine motor tasks, including fastening buttons, typing, and handling cutlery. She also reports that her legs feel heavy on stairs. She has intermittent numbness in both arms, but it does not follow one clear dermatomal or peripheral nerve distribution.

She has no significant neck pain. She denies falls, but reports that her balance feels slightly less reliable. Over the past 2 months, she has noticed mild bladder urgency, which she has attributed to possible urinary tract infections.

Her current working diagnosis is bilateral carpal tunnel syndrome.

<ReasoningPrompt question="What is your leading concern, and why should you not simply continue with the planned pre-operative CTS pathway?" />

<RevealAnswer>
The leading concern is degenerative cervical myelopathy.

The planned pathway is bilateral carpal tunnel release, but the full clinical picture is not explained by carpal tunnel syndrome. Bilateral hand symptoms could fit CTS, but CTS does not explain leg heaviness, gait change, bladder urgency, brisk reflexes, Hoffmann’s signs, or possible upper motor neuron involvement.

This is the central reasoning point: the referral label should not override the clinical assessment. The patient needs screening for cervical cord involvement before proceeding with a peripheral nerve surgery pathway. </RevealAnswer>

## Diagnostic trap

<ReasoningPrompt question="Why is this case easy to misclassify as bilateral carpal tunnel syndrome?" />

<RevealAnswer>
It is easy to misclassify because bilateral hand symptoms are common in carpal tunnel syndrome, and the patient has already been placed on a CTS surgical pathway.

However, the discriminating features sit outside a wrist-level median nerve problem. Progressive fine motor difficulty, bilateral dexterity loss, leg heaviness, balance change, bladder urgency, and upper motor neuron signs point toward possible spinal cord involvement.

A convenient diagnosis can become dangerous if it stops the clinician from asking whether all symptoms fit. </RevealAnswer>

## Differential diagnosis

<ReasoningPrompt question="What differentials should remain in your reasoning, and what features help discriminate them?" />

<RevealAnswer>
Important differentials include degenerative cervical myelopathy, bilateral carpal tunnel syndrome, peripheral neuropathy, cervical radiculopathy, multiple sclerosis, motor neurone disease, and other neurological disease.

Bilateral carpal tunnel syndrome may explain hand paraesthesia, but it does not explain leg symptoms, gait disturbance, bladder urgency, or upper motor neuron signs.

Peripheral neuropathy can be bilateral, but it would more typically present with a peripheral pattern and does not usually explain clear upper motor neuron findings.

Cervical radiculopathy can cause arm symptoms, but it usually follows a nerve root pattern and does not explain lower limb involvement or cord signs.

A broader neurological disorder should remain in mind if symptoms are disseminated, progressive, or not structurally consistent. </RevealAnswer>

## Red flag and referral reasoning

<ReasoningPrompt question="Which features make this a high-stakes referral case?" />

<RevealAnswer>
High-stakes features include progressive bilateral hand dysfunction, leg heaviness, balance change, bladder urgency, brisk reflexes, positive Hoffmann’s signs, equivocal Babinski response, and a presentation that is not consistent with CTS alone.

These findings suggest possible cervical cord involvement. This should not be managed as a routine pre-operative CTS assessment. The planned CTS pathway should be paused until cervical myelopathy has been assessed and excluded or confirmed.

The priority is urgent communication with the GP/referring clinician and relevant surgical team, with appropriate referral for cervical spine imaging and specialist review according to local pathways. </RevealAnswer>

<RedFlag title="Possible degenerative cervical myelopathy">
Progressive hand clumsiness, gait or balance change, lower limb symptoms, bladder change, and upper motor neuron signs should prompt urgent consideration of cervical myelopathy and appropriate referral.
</RedFlag>

## Objective assessment

<ReasoningPrompt question="What would you prioritise in the objective assessment?" />

<RevealAnswer>
This presentation demands a myelopathy screen before any routine pre-operative intervention.

Priorities include gait observation, tandem gait, upper and lower limb neurological examination, reflexes, Hoffmann’s sign, Babinski response, clonus where appropriate, hand dexterity testing, rapid alternating hand movements, Romberg test where appropriate, and functional questioning around balance, falls, stairs, buttons, handwriting, typing, and bladder or bowel change.

CTS testing may still be performed, but negative Phalen’s and Durkan’s tests would further weaken the CTS explanation. Even if CTS signs were present, they would not explain the full clinical picture. </RevealAnswer>

## Expected findings

<ReasoningPrompt question="What findings would support your concern for early DCM?" />

<RevealAnswer>
Findings supporting early DCM include mildly slow gait, unsteady tandem gait, reduced hand dexterity bilaterally, difficulty with rapid alternating hand movements, brisk biceps reflexes bilaterally, brisk lower limb reflexes, ankle hyperreflexia, positive Hoffmann’s signs, and equivocal or positive Babinski response.

The key pattern is not one isolated test. It is the combination of bilateral hand dysfunction, lower limb involvement, gait change, bladder symptoms, and upper motor neuron signs. </RevealAnswer>

## Management reasoning

<ReasoningPrompt question="What should your immediate management focus on?" />

<RevealAnswer>
Immediate management should focus on stopping the routine pre-operative pathway, explaining the concern clearly, arranging urgent medical/specialist review, and communicating findings to the GP and referring surgical team.

This is not a strengthening, splinting, or routine CTS education session. The clinical priority is to avoid proceeding with the wrong pathway before possible cord involvement has been investigated.

Safety-netting should include advice to seek urgent medical help if there is rapid worsening, new falls, increasing weakness, worsening gait, new bowel or bladder dysfunction, or rapidly progressive neurological symptoms. </RevealAnswer>

## Communication

<ReasoningPrompt question="How would you explain the concern without causing unnecessary alarm?" />

<RevealAnswer>
A balanced explanation could be:

“Before we continue with the pre-operative carpal tunnel assessment, I want to explain something I have found today. Some of your symptoms and examination findings suggest there may be involvement of the spinal cord in your neck, rather than this being only a wrist nerve problem. I am not saying that is confirmed today, but it is important enough that we should pause the planned pathway and make sure the right scan and specialist review are arranged.” </RevealAnswer>

## Common traps

<RevealAnswer title="Common traps in this case">
Common traps include accepting the referral diagnosis without reassessment, focusing only on the hands, failing to ask about gait and bladder symptoms, missing upper motor neuron signs, and assuming lack of neck pain rules out cervical myelopathy.

Another major trap is proceeding with CTS surgery planning when the presentation contains features that CTS cannot explain. </RevealAnswer>
