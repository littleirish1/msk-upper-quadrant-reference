## Scenario history

**Margaret, 63, retired teacher.** 4-month progressive history of "clumsy hands" — difficulty doing buttons, dropping cups. Mild neck stiffness but denies significant pain. Husband reports she has started shuffling when she walks and nearly fell twice last week.

New urinary urgency over the past 6 weeks. No trauma. No fever or weight loss.

## Scenario examination / objective findings

Gait: slow, broad-based. Tandem gait: unable to complete. Upper limbs: bilateral grip weakness, hand clumsiness on rapid alternating movements. Reflexes: brisk throughout bilaterally. Hoffmann's sign: positive bilaterally. Babinski: positive right. Clonus: sustained at right ankle.

## Safety flags / red flags

Progressive bilateral neurological deficit. UMN signs. Bladder involvement. Gait disturbance. ALL are red flags.

## Legacy diagnosis

Degenerative Cervical Myelopathy (DCM)

## Legacy reasoning

Bilateral progressive hand dysfunction + gait ataxia + UMN signs (Hoffmann's, Babinski, hyperreflexia, clonus) + bladder involvement = cord dysfunction until proven otherwise. Not radiculopathy (bilateral, UMN pattern). Not peripheral neuropathy (UMN signs, gait). Urgent specialist referral + MRI required.

## Clinical traps

Treating this as bilateral CTS or degenerative hand changes without completing a UMN screen. The most common diagnostic trap for DCM in clinical practice (RADCeM-physio data) is attributing bilateral hand dysfunction to peripheral causes. A good answer identifies progressive bilateral symptoms; an outstanding answer explains why the leg heaviness and bladder urgency CANNOT be explained by CTS and why this changes the entire clinical priority.

## Legacy domain content

### 1. Most Likely Diagnosis

- Icon: 🔵
- Badge: db-diag

**Degenerative Cervical Myelopathy (DCM).**

This presentation is highly concerning for spinal cord dysfunction because symptoms are bilateral, progressive, and affect dexterity and balance. Difficulty with buttons, dropping objects, hand clumsiness, gait disturbance, and urinary urgency point toward cord involvement rather than isolated nerve root compression.

### 2. Differential Diagnoses

- Icon: 🔵
- Badge: db-diag

- **Peripheral neuropathy** — could explain bilateral sensory symptoms but would not account for UMN signs or characteristic gait change
- **Cervical radiculopathy** — more likely if unilateral and root-specific
- **Other neurological pathology** — MS or central neurological disease depending on wider history and findings

### 3. Objective Assessment

- Icon: 🟡
- Badge: db-obj

A 90% answer should **prioritise safety and efficiency.**

- Gait observation; tandem gait if safe
- Upper limb myotomes, dermatomes, reflexes
- Lower limb reflexes if indicated
- **Hoffmann's sign**
- **Babinski sign**
- **Clonus**
- Romberg / balance test where safe
- Observation of hand function and dexterity

**✦ Interpretation**

Hyperreflexia + positive Hoffmann's + positive Babinski + clonus + impaired tandem gait + bilateral hand dysfunction = strongly raise suspicion of myelopathy.

### 4. Red Flags / Safety

- Icon: 🔴
- Badge: db-safety

**⚠ This is a RED FLAG Presentation**

Delayed recognition risks ongoing spinal cord compromise and irreversible outcome.

**Immediate priorities:**

- Explain concern clearly
- DO NOT delay referral with a trial of treatment
- Document findings thoroughly
- Initiate urgent onward referral according to local pathway
- Urgent MRI directly affects diagnosis, referral urgency, and management

### 5. Management

- Icon: 🟡
- Badge: db-mgmt

**This is not a routine conservative physiotherapy case.**

- Explain concern clearly — avoid delaying with symptomatic treatment
- Urgent specialist referral (neurosurgery/neurology)
- Urgent MRI
- Document all findings
- Safety-net for sudden deterioration (acute weakness, loss of bladder/bowel)
- Advise: avoid falls risk, avoid spinal manipulation

### 6. Communication

- Icon: 🟣
- Badge: db-comm

**90% Model Language**

"Some of your symptoms suggest the spinal cord may be under pressure rather than this being a simple mechanical neck problem. Because of that, I think you need urgent specialist assessment and imaging."

This is clear, safe, and non-alarmist. Check the patient's understanding and manage anxiety with professional calm.

### 7. Evidence Base

- Icon: 🩷
- Badge: db-evid

Advanced-practice clinicians are expected to recognise progressive bilateral neurological symptoms and expedite referral. Routine MSK treatment is inappropriate when myelopathy is suspected.

**Module Reading List**

- **Fehlings MG et al, Global Spine J (2017)** DOI:10.1177/2192568217702914 — CPG for DCM management: mild/moderate/severe disease. Surgical intervention supported to prevent irreversible cord damage
- **Smith SS, Stewart ME, Davies BM, Kotter MRN** — Prevalence of asymptomatic and symptomatic spinal cord compression on MRI: SR+MA. MRI compression ≠ automatic symptomatic DCM — clinical correlation is essential
- **Tetreault LA, Kopjar B, Côté P, Fehlings MG et al, Spine J (2015)** — Clinical prediction model for surgical outcome, 743 patients (AOSpine datasets)
- **Tetreault L, Wilson JR, Fehlings MG et al** — Preoperative symptom duration predicts functional outcome post-surgery — directly justifies urgency of referral

**⭐ Outstanding Nuance**

Kotter et al: cord compression on MRI is common in asymptomatic individuals. The diagnosis of DCM requires both imaging evidence AND clinical signs. This is why the objective examination is essential — imaging alone does not make the diagnosis.
