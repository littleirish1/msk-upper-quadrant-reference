## Scenario history

**Declan, 36, CrossFit coach.** 8 days ago: woke at 3am with 10/10 severe left shoulder/upper arm pain — "burning and electric." Called emergency GP. Pain now 3/10 but progressive weakness raising arm noted. Visible scapular winging noticed by colleague. Had a chest infection 4 weeks ago. No trauma. No neck symptoms. First presentation to physiotherapy.

## Scenario examination / objective findings

Scapular winging at rest, prominent on wall push-up test. Shoulder ABD: 2/5 left. ER: 3/5. Serratus anterior: markedly weak. Sensation: patchy reduced over lateral upper arm and forearm — no clear dermatomal pattern. Cervical AROM: full, pain-free, no arm symptoms reproduced. Reflexes: biceps mildly reduced. ULTT1: mildly sensitised bilaterally (non-specific).

## Safety flags / red flags

No UMN features. Non-dermatomal pattern = not a root lesion. Rapid onset severe neuropathic pain without trauma = not a mechanical shoulder problem.

## Legacy diagnosis

Parsonage-Turner Syndrome (Neuralgic Amyotrophy) — Long thoracic nerve most affected

## Legacy reasoning

Classic triad: sudden severe nocturnal neuropathic pain → resolution of pain → emerging weakness and wasting. Scapular winging = serratus anterior (long thoracic nerve). Patchy non-dermatomal sensory loss distinguishes from C5/6 radiculopathy. Precipitating viral illness is common. Van Alfen 2006 (Brain): 246 cases confirm this pattern. Prognosis: ~75% meaningful recovery at 3 years.

## Clinical traps

Starting aggressive strengthening exercises in the acute phase — the nerve needs to recover before the muscle can respond to loading. A good answer gives a rehabilitation programme. An outstanding answer is explicit that early aggressive strengthening is inappropriate, explains the nerve recovery timeline (pain first, then weakness over weeks), and targets movement quality and coordination of the recovering muscles rather than force production.

## Legacy domain content

### 1. Most Likely Diagnosis

- Icon: 🔵
- Badge: db-diag

**Parsonage-Turner Syndrome (neuralgic amyotrophy) — brachial neuritis.**

A 36-year-old male CrossFit coach presenting 8 days after sudden-onset severe left shoulder and upper arm pain that woke him from sleep. Pain now reducing but he has noticed progressive weakness raising his left arm. Scapular winging visible from behind. Had a chest infection 4 weeks ago treated with antibiotics. No cervical symptoms. No trauma.

### 2. Differential Diagnoses

- Icon: 🔵
- Badge: db-diag

- **Massive rotator cuff tear** — would show weakness but no scapular winging; acute severe nocturnal neuropathic pain is atypical for cuff
- **Cervical radiculopathy C5/C6** — neck movement would reproduce symptoms; dermatomal pattern
- **Serratus anterior strain** — possible with physical training but doesn't explain the acute pain pattern or non-traumatic mechanism
- **Brachial plexus injury** — would require significant trauma mechanism

### 3. Objective Assessment

- Icon: 🟡
- Badge: db-obj

- Observation: scapular winging at rest, worse on wall press
- AROM: limited by weakness not pain
- PROM: full and pain-free
- Manual muscle testing: serratus anterior — markedly weak; shoulder ABD/ER weakness
- Sensation: patchy, non-dermatomal pattern
- Reflexes: mildly reduced biceps (non-specific)
- Cervical AROM: full, pain-free, no arm symptoms reproduced
- Neurodynamic testing: mildly sensitised, non-specific

**✦ Discriminating Feature**

Patchy, non-dermatomal sensory pattern + scapular winging + no cervical provocation = NOT a root problem. This is peripheral nerve involvement with a nerve-specific distribution, not dermatomal.

### 4. Red Flags / Safety

- Icon: 🔴
- Badge: db-safety

**Escalate If**

- Bilateral involvement
- Respiratory compromise (phrenic nerve involvement)
- No improvement in 3+ months — EMG indicated
- Diagnostic uncertainty — specialist opinion
- Cases presenting before correct diagnosis who undergo surgery — Gabet 2023: significant number of PTS patients have had surgery before correct diagnosis

### 5. Management

- Icon: 🟡
- Badge: db-mgmt

- **Acute phase:** Pain management (NSAIDs, opioids if severe), sling for comfort, energy conservation
- Steroids — high dose within first days of onset (?IVG) — both lacking strong evidence but may be considered early
- Advise on natural history — pain typically reduces first; weakness may persist months-years
- **Subacute/chronic rehabilitation:** Scapular control, movement quality, serratus anterior facilitation, graduated loading as nerve function returns
- Do NOT commence early aggressive strengthening — nerve recovery precedes functional muscle use
- Surgery: emerging evidence; reserved for those not improving with conservative methods

### 6. Communication

- Icon: 🟣
- Badge: db-comm

**Example language**

"What you've experienced is a condition called Parsonage-Turner Syndrome — it's an inflammatory reaction affecting the nerves that supply your shoulder and upper arm. It's more common than people think and is often missed. The severe pain you had at the start is typical — and it usually does reduce, as yours has. The weakness and winging of your shoulder blade will likely improve over time, but it can be a slow process — months, sometimes longer. Our focus in rehabilitation is on keeping your movement patterns healthy while those nerves recover."

### 7. Evidence Base

- Icon: 🩷
- Badge: db-evid

**Key Evidence**

- **Van Alfen N, Brain (2006)** — 246 cases: 90%+ acute severe pain; weakness 1-4 weeks later; bilateral ~30%; recovery: ~36% complete at 1yr, ~75% at 3yr
- **Gabet JM, Muscle & Nerve (2023)** — Contemporary evaluation, diagnosis and treatment; significant proportion have surgery before correct diagnosis
- **Yoon AP et al.** — Predisposing factors for incomplete recovery: older age, severe initial weakness, multiple nerve involvement, delayed diagnosis

**⭐ Outstanding — Prognosis with Nuance**

Don't just say "recovery is often good." Van Alfen gives you the numbers: ~75% at 3 years. But Yoon identifies who does worse: older age, multiple nerve involvement, severe initial weakness, delayed diagnosis. This patient is 36 with apparently isolated long thoracic nerve involvement and early presentation — his prognosis is more favourable. State this explicitly.
