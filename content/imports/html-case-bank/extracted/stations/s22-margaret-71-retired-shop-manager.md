## Scenario history

**Margaret, 71, retired shop manager.** 3 days ago: severe thoracic pain onset while reaching into overhead cupboard. Felt something "give." Pain 8/10, sharp, mechanical (worse with movement, rising from sitting, lying flat). Long-term prednisolone for polymyalgia rheumatica. DEXA 2 years ago: T-score -2.6 (osteoporosis). No neurological symptoms. No cancer history. No unexplained weight loss. No fever.

## Scenario examination / objective findings

Observation: increased thoracic kyphosis. Guarded posture. Percussion test: focal bony tenderness T9-T10. Minimal movement assessment — all thoracic movements severely limited and painful. Brief lower limb neurological screen: unremarkable. No bowel/bladder deficit.

## Safety flags / red flags

TUNAFISH: S (steroid use), A (age >50), T (minor trauma). Multiple flags present. VCF until proven otherwise.

## Legacy diagnosis

Osteoporotic Vertebral Compression Fracture — T9/T10

## Legacy reasoning

TUNAFISH triple flag: steroid use + age >50 + minor mechanism. Positive percussion test (NASS Grade B evidence). Known DEXA -2.6. No neurological deficit — uncomplicated VCF. Priority: imaging (X-ray first; MRI if cord involvement or pathological fracture suspected), pain management, fracture liaison, bone protection. VERTOS IV: vertebroplasty no significant benefit over sham — conservative management is first-line.

## Clinical traps

Over-examining an acute osteoporotic fracture — applying full active movement assessment when percussion tenderness and mechanism already suggest VCF risks causing harm. The outstanding answer modifies the assessment approach based on the suspected diagnosis before full assessment is complete. Also: not considering whether this is a pathological fracture — the outstanding answer states pathological fracture must be excluded on imaging.

## Legacy domain content

### 1. Most Likely Diagnosis

- Icon: 🔵
- Badge: db-diag

**Osteoporotic vertebral compression fracture (VCF) — likely T9-T10.**

A 71-year-old woman prescribed long-term prednisolone for polymyalgia rheumatica. Presented with sudden onset severe thoracic pain while reaching into an overhead cupboard 3 days ago. Reports she felt something "give" in her back. Pain 8/10, sharp, mechanical (worse with movement), worse lying flat and rising from sitting. DEXA 2 years ago: T-score -2.6 (osteoporosis). No neurological symptoms.

### 2. Differential Diagnoses

- Icon: 🔵
- Badge: db-diag

- **Pathological fracture** — if cancer history, constitutional symptoms, or unusual imaging features → exclude malignancy
- **Metastatic lesion** — if known malignancy or TUNAFISH flags
- **Thoracic muscle strain** — would not explain bony percussion tenderness in an osteoporotic patient
- **Insufficiency fracture** — same category as osteoporotic VCF; overlap

### 3. Objective Assessment

- Icon: 🟡
- Badge: db-obj

**Approach with caution — modify assessment based on clinical suspicion.**

- Observation only initially — posture, kyphosis, guarding
- Closed-fist percussion test (positive = bony tenderness) — Grade B evidence for VCF (NASS guidelines)
- Supine sign (can patient transfer to supine without significant difficulty?)
- Back pain inducing test
- Brief neurological screen — lower limbs (rule out cord/cauda equina involvement)
- Avoid aggressive ROM testing — modify to match severity

**✦ 90% Note**

TUNAFISH flags present: Steroid use (S), Age >50 (A), Trauma though minor (T). The examination approach must reflect the clinical concern — excessive loading in an acute VCF can cause harm.

### 4. Red Flags / Safety

- Icon: 🔴
- Badge: db-safety

**TUNAFISH: S + A + T**

Long-term steroid use (S) + age >50 (A) + minor trauma mechanism (T) = VCF until proven otherwise. Imaging changes management, safety advice, and onward referral.

**Urgent if:** neurological deficit, rapid deterioration, concern for pathological fracture, or acute cord compression.

### 5. Management

- Icon: 🟡
- Badge: db-mgmt

- X-ray first line; MRI if cord involvement suspected, pathological fracture to exclude, or acute injury on background of previous fracture
- Pain management: paracetamol, NSAIDs (with caution — GI/renal/cardiac in elderly), short-term opioids if needed
- Fracture liaison referral — secondary fracture prevention
- Bone health assessment: DEXA if not recent, calcium and vitamin D, bone protection medication review
- NOGG guidelines: pain management, exercise and rehabilitation, pharmacological management, secondary fracture prevention
- Rehabilitation: Strong Steady Straight programme; postural work; graduated activity resumption
- Surgical: vertebroplasty/kyphoplasty — select cases only (VERTOS IV: no significant benefit of vertebroplasty over sham for pain relief)
- Safety netting: return if neurological symptoms develop, rapid worsening, or new fracture suspected

### 6. Communication

- Icon: 🟣
- Badge: db-comm

**Outstanding Language**

"Based on your examination and your history — particularly the long-term steroid use and your bone density result — I'm very concerned that you may have a fracture in your spine, even though there was no big accident. This can happen with osteoporosis even with very minor movements. I'm going to arrange a scan as a priority. In the meantime I want to give you some advice about how to keep yourself safe and manage the pain — and we'll need to talk about your bone health to help prevent this happening again."

### 7. Evidence Base

- Icon: 🩷
- Badge: db-evid

**Key Evidence**

- **NASS Evidence-Based Guidelines** — Closed-fist percussion, supine sign, back pain inducing test: Grade B recommendation for identifying osteoporotic VCF
- **NOGG (National Osteoporosis Guideline Group UK)** — Pain management, exercise, pharmacological, secondary prevention
- **VERTOS IV Trial (BMJ)** — Vertebroplasty vs sham: no statistically significant difference in pain relief at 1 or 12 months
- **Almy A et al, JAMA Network Open (2024)** — Conservative treatments for osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures: SR + network meta-analysis

**⭐ Outstanding — VERTOS IV Nuance**

VERTOS IV showed no significant benefit of vertebroplasty over sham. An outstanding answer doesn't just cite this — it explains the implications: surgery is not first-line, patient selection is everything, and the evidence supports conservative management as the primary approach. For patients in whom conservative management fails (severe persistent pain, functional decline, progressive kyphosis), the discussion is more nuanced — shared decision-making applies.
