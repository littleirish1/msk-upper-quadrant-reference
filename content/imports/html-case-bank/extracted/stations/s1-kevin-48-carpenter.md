## Scenario history

**Kevin, 47, carpenter.** 6-week history of right-sided neck pain radiating down the lateral forearm to his thumb and index finger. Intermittent pins and needles in the same distribution. Pain is 7/10 at worst. He notices weakness when gripping his tools and difficulty turning a screwdriver.

Aggravated by looking up at work and turning head right. Partially relieved by resting his arm on his head (he found this himself). Sleep disrupted. No bilateral symptoms. No change in walking or balance.

## Scenario examination / objective findings

Cervical AROM: extension and right rotation limited and painful, reproducing arm symptoms. Neurological exam: reduced sensation lateral forearm and thumb; elbow flexion strength 4/5 right vs 5/5 left; biceps reflex reduced right side. Spurling's test: positive right — concordant arm pain. Cervical distraction: partial relief. ULTT median bias: positive right at 40° elbow extension vs 70° left.

## Safety flags / red flags

No bilateral symptoms. No gait change. No bowel/bladder symptoms. No fever, weight loss, cancer history.

## Legacy diagnosis

Cervical Radiculopathy — most likely C6 nerve root

## Legacy reasoning

Dermatomal arm pain to thumb/index = C6 territory. Reduced biceps reflex = C6. Elbow flexion weakness = C6 myotome. Positive Wainner cluster (Spurling's + ULTT + distraction relief + rotation <60° ipsilateral). Relief with arm abduction (Bakody sign) = foraminal decompression. No UMN signs — cord not involved.

## Clinical traps

Stopping after identifying radiculopathy without screening for myelopathy. Treating a C6 radiculopathy in a patient who also has bilateral hand clumsiness or gait change — those features don't fit. A good answer names C6; an outstanding answer asks WHY the pattern is unilateral and considers whether there's a cord component.

## Legacy domain content

### 1. Most Likely Diagnosis

- Icon: 🔵
- Badge: db-diag

**Right cervical radiculopathy, most likely C6 nerve root involvement.**

This is the most consistent diagnosis because pain radiates in a dermatomal distribution involving the lateral forearm, thumb and index finger. There is paraesthesia and reduced functional strength, suggesting nerve root dysfunction rather than simple somatic referred pain. Aggravation with cervical extension and ipsilateral rotation is consistent with foraminal narrowing; relief with shoulder abduction or distraction further supports radicular involvement.

### 2. Differential Diagnoses

- Icon: 🔵
- Badge: db-diag

- **Cervical radicular pain without neurological deficit** — if arm pain in a nerve root distribution but no objective sensory loss or reflex change
- **Median nerve entrapment / CTS** — if symptoms predominantly distal in hand, nocturnal, provoked at wrist, not clearly linked to neck movement
- **RCRSP** — if localised to shoulder/upper arm, painful arc, normal neurological examination
- **Thoracic outlet syndrome** — if posture/overhead dependent with diffuse arm heaviness or vascular features rather than a clear dermatomal pattern

### 3. Objective Assessment

- Icon: 🟡
- Badge: db-obj

- **Cervical active movement:** Assess all planes. Expect painful, limited extension and ipsilateral rotation with possible arm symptom reproduction
- **Full neurological exam:** Myotomes, dermatomes, reflexes + brief myelopathy screen. Expected C6: altered sensation lateral forearm/thumb; weakness elbow flexion and/or wrist extension; reduced biceps reflex
- **Radiculopathy cluster (Wainner):** Spurling's, cervical distraction, ULTT median bias, rotation <60° ipsilateral
- **Brief shoulder screen:** Exclude shoulder-generated pain

**✦ 90% Interpretation**

Relief with distraction supports radiculopathy. Concordant arm pain with Spurling's = nerve root involvement. Positive ULTT = neural mechanosensitivity. No single test is diagnostic — use cluster.

### 4. Red Flags / Safety

- Icon: 🔴
- Badge: db-safety

**Must Screen For**

- Progressive neurological deficit
- Bilateral symptoms
- Gait disturbance / hand clumsiness
- Upper motor neurone signs
- Severe non-mechanical pain
- Systemic: cancer history, fever, unexplained weight loss

**Escalate if:** progressive weakness, widespread neurological signs, suspected myelopathy, or suspicion of serious pathology.

### 5. Investigations

- Icon: 🩷
- Badge: db-evid

Routine imaging is **not immediately required** in a coherent early radiculopathy presentation without red flags.

**MRI indicated if:** progressive deficit; failure to improve with appropriate conservative management; myelopathy suspected; serious pathology suspected; specialist intervention being considered.

### 6. Management Plan

- Icon: 🟡
- Badge: db-mgmt

- **Education and reassurance:** Symptoms consistent with nerve root irritation/dysfunction — many cases improve conservatively
- **Activity modification:** Reduce prolonged extension, overhead work, sustained end-range rotation
- **Symptom modification:** Supported arm position, unloading strategies where helpful
- **Exercise:** Cervical movement, deep neck flexor control, scapular and upper quarter rehab, graded upper limb loading, neurodynamic exercise where appropriate
- **Manual therapy:** Adjunct only — not the sole intervention
- **Review:** Monitor neurological status, irritability, sleep, function, 24-hour response

### 7. Communication

- Icon: 🟣
- Badge: db-comm

Explain clearly that symptoms are consistent with irritation of a cervical nerve root and that many cases improve with conservative treatment.

Advise the patient which symptoms require urgent reassessment:

- Worsening weakness
- Bilateral symptoms
- Gait change

**Example language**

"The pain going down your arm appears to be coming from where a nerve exits your neck. That's what's causing the numbness in your thumb and fingers. Most people with this do improve with the right exercises and guidance — but I want you to contact us urgently if you notice weakness getting worse, symptoms in both arms, or any change in your walking."

### 8. Evidence Base

- Icon: 🩷
- Badge: db-evid

Justify conservative care, selective imaging, and multimodal rehabilitation. Manual therapy is best used as part of a broader active management plan rather than as a standalone solution. High-level answers reference the Wainner (2003) diagnostic cluster for cervical radiculopathy.

**Module Reading List**

- **NICE CKS (2023)** — Management of cervical radicular pain/radiculopathy. Full pathway: conservative management, imaging thresholds, escalation criteria
- **Kjær P et al, Eur Spine J (2017)** DOI:10.1007/s00586-017-5121-8 — National clinical guidelines for non-surgical treatment of cervical radiculopathy
- **BASS (2021)** — Surgical options: ACDF (disc + degenerative change) vs ACDR (disc prolapse without degeneration — maintains movement, reduces adjacent segment load)

**⭐ Outstanding Nuance**

Wainner cluster (2003): 3+ positive tests = high PPV, but developed in a specific clinical population. Acknowledge that test accuracy is context-dependent. Spurling's specificity ~93% but sensitivity only ~30% — a negative test does not rule out radiculopathy.
