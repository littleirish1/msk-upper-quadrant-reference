## Scenario history

**James, 41, GP.** 10 days ago: sudden-onset severe right shoulder and upper arm pain that woke him from sleep — "worst pain of my life," 10/10. Pain now reducing to 4/10. However, over the last 5 days he has noticed progressive weakness raising his arm and difficulty abducting the shoulder. Right scapular winging noted by his wife.

No trauma. Had a flu-like illness 3 weeks ago. No cervical symptoms. Neurological exam by colleague: right shoulder abduction 2/5, serratus anterior weakness confirmed.

## Scenario examination / objective findings

Observation: right scapular winging at rest, worse on wall press. Shoulder ABD: 2/5. ER: 3/5. Serratus anterior: significantly weak. Sensation: patchy reduced sensation over lateral arm and forearm — does NOT follow a dermatomal pattern. Cervical AROM: full, pain-free, no arm symptoms reproduced. Reflexes: biceps slightly reduced right.

## Safety flags / red flags

No UMN signs. No bilateral involvement. Patchy non-dermatomal pattern — not a cord or root problem.

## Legacy diagnosis

Neuralgic Amyotrophy (Parsonage-Turner Syndrome)

## Legacy reasoning

Classic triad: acute severe neuropathic pain → partial resolution → weakness and wasting emerging. Scapular winging = long thoracic nerve (serratus anterior). Patchy, non-dermatomal distribution distinguishes from radiculopathy. Precipitating viral illness is common. van Alfen (Brain 2006): 246 cases confirm this pattern. Not reproduced by cervical movement.

## Clinical traps

Attributing the weakness to the rotator cuff or cervical radiculopathy without considering the temporal sequence. The hallmark of PTS is the sequence: sudden severe neuropathic pain → pain reduces → weakness and wasting emerge. If the clinician focuses on the current presentation (weakness + scapular winging) without taking a detailed history of the acute pain phase, they will miss the diagnosis. Good answers identify scapular winging; outstanding answers explain the long thoracic nerve anatomy and distinguish from C5/6 radiculopathy by the non-dermatomal sensory pattern.

## Legacy domain content

### 1. Most Likely Diagnosis

- Icon: 🔵
- Badge: db-diag

**Neuralgic amyotrophy (Parsonage–Turner Syndrome).**

Classic pattern: sudden severe shoulder/upper arm pain → weakness and wasting emerge → patchy nerve involvement → possible scapular winging. Does not fit routine root or shoulder problem. Long thoracic and suprascapular nerves commonly involved.

### 2. Differential Diagnoses

- Icon: 🔵
- Badge: db-diag

- Cervical radiculopathy
- Traumatic brachial plexus injury
- Rotator cuff tear
- Peripheral nerve entrapment

### 3. Objective Assessment

- Icon: 🟡
- Badge: db-obj

- Detailed neuro exam
- Scapular observation — winging assessment
- Muscle testing across involved nerves
- Functional assessment

### 4. Investigations & Evidence

- Icon: 🩷
- Badge: db-evid

Electrodiagnostic studies (EMG/NCS) and advanced imaging may help confirm diagnosis and identify more significant structural nerve changes. Useful when diagnosis is uncertain or recovery is poor.

**Module Reading List**

- **van Alfen N, Brain (2006)** DOI:10.1093/brain/awh722 — Landmark: clinical spectrum in 246 cases. 90%+ had acute severe pain; weakness emerged 1-4 weeks later; bilateral in ~30%; ~36% complete recovery at 1yr, ~75% at 3yr
- **Gabet JM, Muscle & Nerve** DOI:10.1002/mus.28274 — Contemporary evaluation, diagnosis and treatment update (AANEM CME)
- **Yoon AP, Lee EY, Tagliero LE, Pulos N, Spinner RJ, Bishop AT, Shin A** — Predisposing factors for incomplete spontaneous recovery: older age, severe initial weakness, multiple nerve involvement, delayed diagnosis

**⭐ Outstanding — Prognosis Nuance**

Van Alfen 2006 gives you the numbers: ~75% meaningful recovery by 3 years. But Yoon et al identify who does worse. A distinction answer doesn't say "recovery is often good" — it says "recovery is good in ~75% at 3 years, but this patient's [multiple nerve involvement / delayed presentation] suggests a more cautious prognosis."

### 5. Management

- Icon: 🟡
- Badge: db-mgmt

A high-mark answer should **avoid inappropriate early strengthening focus.**

- Pain management in acute phase
- Movement coordination retraining
- Scapular control
- Energy conservation and fatigue management
- Targeted rehab based on weakness pattern
- Specialist opinion if poor recovery persists

### 6. Communication

- Icon: 🟣
- Badge: db-comm

Explain that recovery can be prolonged and incomplete, and that rehab focuses on movement quality and function rather than just force production.

**Example language**

"This condition affects the nerves that supply your shoulder and arm. The pain usually settles over time, but the weakness can take longer to recover — sometimes months to a couple of years. Our focus is on keeping your movement patterns healthy while those nerves recover."
