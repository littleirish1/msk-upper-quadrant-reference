## Scenario history

**Siobhan, 42, physiotherapist (off work).** 6 weeks ago: woke at 3am with 10/10 excruciating right shoulder and upper arm pain — burning and electric. Pain now 4/10 and reducing. Over past 2 weeks: "shoulder muscle wasting" and difficulty lifting arm. Currently listed for shoulder arthroscopy by orthopaedic team ("rotator cuff tear with impingement"). GI illness 4 weeks before onset.

## Scenario examination / objective findings

Scapular winging at rest. Shoulder ABD: 2/5. ER: 3/5. Serratus anterior markedly weak (wall push-up: winging prominent). PROM: FULL and PAIN-FREE in all directions. Sensation: patchy reduction lateral upper arm and mid-forearm — NOT dermatomal. Cervical AROM: full, pain-free, no arm reproduction. Reflexes: mildly reduced biceps right.

## Safety flags / red flags

FULL passive ROM pain-free = NOT shoulder primary condition. Patchy non-dermatomal sensory loss = NOT radiculopathy. Pain-then-weakness temporal sequence = PTS. Planned surgery is inappropriate.

## Legacy diagnosis

Parsonage-Turner Syndrome (PTS) — Misdiagnosed as Rotator Cuff Tear

## Legacy reasoning

Classic PTS triad: sudden severe nocturnal neuropathic pain (10/10, burning) → pain reduces → weakness and wasting emerge. Scapular winging = long thoracic nerve (serratus anterior). PROM FULL and PAIN-FREE = not shoulder primary (RC tear/impingement always has some passive ROM discomfort). Patchy non-dermatomal = peripheral nerve not root. Post-GI illness = viral trigger.

## Clinical traps

Accepting the orthopaedic "rotator cuff tear" diagnosis without applying pattern recognition. The single most discriminating finding: PROM is full and pain-free. Outstanding answer uses this finding to reframe the entire diagnostic picture and communicates concerns to the surgeon urgently.

## Legacy domain content

### 1. Most Likely Diagnosis

- Icon: 🔵
- Badge: db-diag

**Parsonage-Turner Syndrome (neuralgic amyotrophy) — misdiagnosed as shoulder pathology.**

This station specifically targets the most common misdiagnosis pattern. PTS is often attributed to cervical radiculopathy, rotator cuff tear, or frozen shoulder. The discriminating features are the temporal sequence and the non-dermatomal pattern — both features that require active history-taking to elicit.

### 2. What Was the Patient Told Before? Reframing the History

- Icon: 🔵
- Badge: db-diag

- **Previously told: rotator cuff tear:** RC tear shows weakness BUT no acute neuropathic pain phase, no scapular winging, dermatomal or myotomal pattern. PTS: pain first (severe, nocturnal, neuropathic) → pain reduces → weakness emerges.
- **Previously told: C5/C6 radiculopathy:** Radiculopathy: neck movement reproduces concordant arm symptoms; dermatomal sensory loss. PTS: no neck provocation; PATCHY non-dermatomal sensory pattern.
- **Previously told: frozen shoulder:** FS: equal AROM and PROM restriction in capsular pattern; no acute pain-then-weakness sequence. PTS: PROM is FULL and pain-free; weakness is isolated to specific nerve distributions.

### 3. When PTS Has Been Missed

- Icon: 🔴
- Badge: db-safety

**Gabet 2023 — Many PTS Patients Have Surgery Before Correct Diagnosis**

This is the safety point of this station: a significant number of PTS patients undergo shoulder surgery (often arthroscopy or decompression) before the correct diagnosis is established. Awareness of the pattern is the only defence.

If PTS is suspected in a patient who has already been listed for shoulder surgery: communicate concern to the referring surgeon. Request EMG/NCS. Do NOT proceed with pre-operative assessment as planned without addressing the diagnostic uncertainty.

### 4. Examination

- Icon: 🟡
- Badge: db-obj

- Scapular winging — visible at rest, pronounced with wall push-up (long thoracic nerve)
- AIN involvement: Kiloh-Nevin sign — cannot make OK sign (FPL + FDP paralysis)
- Detailed manual muscle testing: patchy, non-dermatomal pattern
- PROM: full and pain-free — distinguishes from frozen shoulder
- Cervical AROM: full, pain-free, no arm symptoms reproduced — distinguishes from radiculopathy
- Sensory: patchy, does NOT follow dermatomal map

### 5. Management

- Icon: 🟡
- Badge: db-mgmt

- Acute phase: pain management (NSAIDs, opioids if severe, neuropathic agents)
- High-dose corticosteroids if very early — limited evidence, most patients not diagnosed early enough
- Rehabilitation: movement quality, scapular control, serratus anterior facilitation, graduated loading as nerve function returns
- Do NOT commence aggressive strengthening before nerve recovery begins
- EMG/NCS: useful for confirming diagnosis and mapping nerve involvement
- Prognosis: Van Alfen 2006 — ~75% meaningful recovery at 3 years; Yoon et al. — multiple nerve involvement, older age, delayed diagnosis worsen prognosis

### 6. Evidence

- Icon: 🩷
- Badge: db-evid

**Key Evidence**

- **Van Alfen N (2006) Brain** — 246 cases: 90%+ acute pain; weakness 1-4 weeks later; bilateral ~30%; ~75% meaningful recovery at 3 years
- **Gabet JM (2023) Muscle & Nerve** — Many patients undergo surgery before correct diagnosis — awareness and pattern recognition are the only defence
