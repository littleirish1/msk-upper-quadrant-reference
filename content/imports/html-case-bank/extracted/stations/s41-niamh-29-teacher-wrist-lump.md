## Scenario history

**Niamh, 29, primary school teacher.** 4-month history of lump on the back of right wrist. Started pea-sized, now cherry-tomato sized. Mild aching 2-3/10 with sustained writing and keyboard work. No trauma. Anxious it might be "something serious." GP said "just a ganglion, nothing to worry about."

## Scenario examination / objective findings

Dorsal wrist: 2cm soft, fluctuant, transilluminable mass over scapholunate region. Mildly tender on pressure. Wrist AROM: full, mildly uncomfortable in full extension. Neurological normal. Grip: full.

## Safety flags / red flags

Size has increased (pea to cherry-tomato). X-ray and USS appropriate at this size before surgical referral. Patient anxiety about malignancy requires direct addressing.

## Legacy diagnosis

Dorsal Wrist Ganglion Cyst

## Legacy reasoning

Soft, fluctuant, transilluminable mass over dorsal wrist at scapholunate region + gradual onset + characteristic appearance = dorsal wrist ganglion. Most common hand/wrist soft tissue mass. Transillumination confirms fluid-filled. However: size has increased — X-ray and USS warranted before surgical referral to exclude underlying OA and confirm diagnosis.

Accepting GP's reassurance without arranging imaging — at cherry-tomato size with growth, imaging is warranted. Also: not addressing her malignancy anxiety directly. Outstanding answer name-checks her concern: "The characteristics are very consistent with a benign ganglion — but we'll arrange a scan to confirm."

## Legacy domain content

### 1. Most Likely Diagnosis

- Icon: 🔵
- Badge: db-diag

**Dorsal wrist ganglion cyst.**

Typically idiopathic; most common soft tissue mass of the hand/wrist. Dorsal wrist ganglion is the most common type (accounts for ~60-70% of all hand/wrist ganglia). Transilluminates with light. May indicate underlying OA — investigate underlying joints first before surgical referral. Some studies suggest hypermobility as a risk factor.

### 2. Key Differentials

- Icon: 🔵
- Badge: db-diag

- **Lipoma:** Softer, more mobile, not transilluminable. Discriminator: transillumination test.
- **Mucous cyst (DIP):** Small cyst over DIP joint — associated with OA. Discriminator: location at DIP, nail changes possible.
- **Giant cell tumour of tendon sheath:** Firm, non-transilluminable, slow-growing. Discriminator: urgent secondary care if sinister features — hardness, rapid growth, associated systemic features.

### 3. Safety — When to Refer

- Icon: 🔴
- Badge: db-safety

**Red Flags Requiring XR/USS Before Referral**

- Large ganglia (cherry tomato size or larger)
- Rapidly growing
- Very painful
- Consider XR and USS before sending to surgeon to exclude sinister pathology
- If painful or causing neurological symptoms → investigate and refer sooner

### 4. Management

- Icon: 🟡
- Badge: db-mgmt

- Watchful waiting: 40-50% of ganglia resolve spontaneously over 3-5 years
- Aspiration: simple, low risk, moderate recurrence rate (~50%)
- Surgical excision: lower recurrence than aspiration but procedural risks — for ganglia causing significant pain or functional limitation
- Address underlying joint pathology (OA) if contributing
- Patient education: explain natural history, options, and recurrence rates honestly

### 5. Evidence

- Icon: 🩷
- Badge: db-evid

Ganglion cysts: watchful waiting is a legitimate first-line approach — many resolve spontaneously. Outstanding answer explains the risk-benefit of aspiration vs surgery vs observation based on size, symptoms, and patient preference. OA investigation before surgical referral — do not just refer for excision without exploring underlying joint pathology first.
